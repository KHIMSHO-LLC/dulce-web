Short, plain definitions of the words you meet with a continuous glucose monitor (CGM): the sensor system, the numbers, the reports and the places you see your glucose. Each entry describes what a term means. Each term's name links to its own entry.

<Callout>
This is a reference, not advice. What a reading or a report means for you, and what to do about it, is for you and your care team.
</Callout>

## The sensor system

<Term id="cgm" name="CGM (continuous glucose monitor)">
A wearable device that measures glucose all day and night and gives a new reading at regular intervals, instead of a single finger-prick value. FreeStyle Libre, Dexcom and Medtronic sensors are CGMs.
</Term>

<Term id="sensor" name="Sensor">
The part of a CGM worn on the body: a thin filament under the skin that measures glucose. A sensor works for a set number of days and then has to be replaced; how many depends on the model. For FreeStyle Libre, the <PageLink to="/tools/sensor-end-date">sensor end date calculator</PageLink> works out when yours ends.
</Term>

<Term id="transmitter" name="Transmitter">
The small electronic part that picks up the sensor's signal and sends the readings on to a phone or reader, usually by Bluetooth. On some systems it is a separate piece attached to the sensor, on others it is built into it.
</Term>

<Term id="reader" name="Reader">
A handheld device made for a CGM that shows the readings, used instead of a phone app or alongside it. Some brands call it a receiver. Some systems offer one, with others the phone is the only screen.
</Term>

<Term id="interstitial-glucose" name="Interstitial glucose">
Glucose in the thin layer of fluid between the body's cells, which is what a CGM measures. It follows blood glucose but not at the same instant, so a sensor reading and a finger-prick value can differ.
</Term>

<Term id="sensor-lag" name="Sensor lag">
The delay between a change in blood glucose and the same change showing up in the sensor reading, because glucose takes time to reach the interstitial fluid. It shows most when glucose is rising or falling quickly.
</Term>

<Term id="warm-up" name="Warm-up period">
The time after a new sensor is applied during which it can't show readings yet. How long it lasts depends on the sensor.
</Term>

<Term id="calibration" name="Calibration">
Entering a finger-prick blood glucose value into a CGM system so that it can adjust its readings. Some sensors ask for it, others are calibrated at the factory and don't. It depends on the sensor.
</Term>

## Reading the numbers

<Term id="trend-arrow" name="Trend arrow">
The arrow next to a reading that shows which way glucose is heading and roughly how fast: steady, rising, falling, or rising or falling quickly. Each sensor brand defines its own arrows.
</Term>

<Term id="rate-of-change" name="Rate of change">
How fast glucose is moving, usually given in mg/dL or mmol/L per minute. A trend arrow is a simplified way of showing it.
</Term>

<Term id="mg-dl" name="mg/dL">
Milligrams per decilitre, one of the two units for glucose. It is used in the United States and in many other countries. To convert to mmol/L, divide by 18.
</Term>

<Term id="mmol-l" name="mmol/L">
Millimoles per litre, the other unit for glucose. It is used in the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, the Nordic countries and others. 1 mmol/L is 18 mg/dL (rounded, as most meters and the Dulce app do). The <PageLink to="/tools/glucose-converter">glucose converter</PageLink> goes both ways.
</Term>

## Ranges and reports

<Term id="tir" name="Time in range (TIR)">
The share of time glucose spends inside a target range, worked out from CGM readings. The International Consensus on Time in Range counts 70 to 180 mg/dL (3.9 to 10.0 mmol/L) as the standard range; a person's own range can be set differently by their care team. Try it with the <PageLink to="/tools/time-in-range-calculator">time in range calculator</PageLink> or read the <ArticleLink to="time-in-range">time in range guide</ArticleLink>.
</Term>

<Term id="time-below-range" name="Time below range (TBR)">
The share of time glucose spends under the bottom of the range. The consensus counts readings below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) and reports those below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) separately.
</Term>

<Term id="time-above-range" name="Time above range (TAR)">
The share of time glucose spends over the top of the range. The consensus counts readings above 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) and reports those above 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) separately.
</Term>

<Term id="gmi" name="GMI (glucose management indicator)">
An estimate of A1C worked out from the average glucose of CGM readings, using a published formula (Bergenstal et al., 2018). It is a calculation from sensor data, not a blood test, so it can differ from a lab A1C. The <PageLink to="/tools/gmi-calculator">GMI calculator</PageLink> shows the formula and a chart.
</Term>

<Term id="a1c" name="HbA1c / A1C">
A blood test that shows how much hemoglobin has bound to glucose, which reflects average glucose over roughly three months. It is reported in % (NGSP) or in mmol/mol (IFCC). The <PageLink to="/tools/a1c-calculator">A1C calculator</PageLink> converts between the units, and the <ArticleLink to="hba1c">A1C guide</ArticleLink> explains the test.
</Term>

<Term id="estimated-a1c" name="Estimated A1C">
An A1C value worked out from sensor readings instead of measured in a lab. Its current name is GMI. A separate, older formula (ADAG, Nathan et al., 2008) goes the other way, from a lab A1C to an estimated average glucose (eAG). Either can differ from the lab result.
</Term>

<Term id="cv" name="Glucose variability (CV)">
How much glucose swings around its average. The usual measure is the coefficient of variation (CV): the standard deviation divided by the average, as a percentage. Two people with the same average can have very different variability. The <PageLink to="/tools/time-in-range-calculator">time in range calculator</PageLink> shows it next to the ranges.
</Term>

## Lows, highs and gaps

<Term id="hypoglycemia" name="Hypoglycemia (low glucose)">
Glucose below the range (also spelled hypoglycaemia). In the consensus bands, below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) is low and below 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) is very low. What a low means for you is something to confirm with your sensor and your care team.
</Term>

<Term id="hyperglycemia" name="Hyperglycemia (high glucose)">
Glucose above the range (also spelled hyperglycaemia). In the consensus bands, above 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) is high and above 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) is very high. What a high means for you is something to confirm with your sensor and your care team.
</Term>

<Term id="compression-low" name="Compression low">
A reading that dips lower than real glucose because pressure on the sensor, for example from lying on that arm, temporarily limits the fluid reaching it. Your sensor maker's guidance says how to confirm a reading.
</Term>

<Term id="signal-loss" name="Signal loss">
A gap in the readings because the sensor and the phone or reader stopped exchanging data, for example out of range, or because of a problem with the sensor or the phone. The <ArticleLink to="libre-signal-loss">signal loss guide</ArticleLink> covers FreeStyle Libre.
</Term>

## Sharing and where you see the number

<Term id="follower" name="Follower / sharing app">
A person who sees someone else's glucose on their own phone, or the app that makes it possible. LibreLinkUp, Dexcom Follow and Dulce's family sharing work this way. In Dulce, followers join through an invite link and the person wearing the sensor can remove them at any time. See the <ArticleLink to="family-sharing">family sharing guide</ArticleLink>.
</Term>

<Term id="nightscout" name="Nightscout">
An open-source web app that stores and shows CGM data on a site you run yourself, fed by an uploader. Dulce can read glucose from a Nightscout site and never writes to it. See the <PageLink to="/sensors">list of sensors Dulce works with</PageLink>.
</Term>

<Term id="live-activity" name="Live Activity">
An iPhone feature that keeps live information on the Lock Screen, and in the Dynamic Island where there is one, updating while the app is closed. Dulce uses it to show the current glucose, the trend and a chart. See the <ArticleLink to="lock-screen">Lock Screen guide</ArticleLink>.
</Term>

<Term id="dynamic-island" name="Dynamic Island">
The pill-shaped area at the top of some iPhone models where iOS shows Live Activities in a compact form. Dulce shows the glucose number there.
</Term>

<Term id="widget" name="Widget">
A small panel on the Home Screen, the Lock Screen or in StandBy that shows information from an app without opening it. Dulce's widgets show the current glucose. See the <ArticleLink to="widgets">widgets guide</ArticleLink>.
</Term>

<Term id="complication" name="Complication">
A small item on an Apple Watch face that shows information from an app at a glance. Dulce's complication puts the glucose on the watch face. See the <ArticleLink to="apple-watch">Apple Watch guide</ArticleLink>.
</Term>

## Insulin words

<Term id="basal-bolus" name="Basal and bolus">
Two words for insulin by its role. Basal is the background insulin that covers the body between meals and overnight; bolus is the insulin taken for a meal or to bring down a high. Dulce's logbook records insulin entries as one of these two kinds. How much to take is for your care team to say.
</Term>

## Where to see these numbers in Dulce

Dulce shows the number your sensor reports on the Lock Screen, in widgets and on Apple Watch, and its Trends tab works out time in range, average glucose and GMI over 7, 14, 30 or 90 days. The <ArticleLink to="trends">Trends guide</ArticleLink> explains each one.

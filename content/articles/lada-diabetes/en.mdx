LADA, or latent autoimmune diabetes in adults, is diabetes that the immune system causes, like type 1, but that starts slowly in adulthood, so it often looks like type 2 at first. Many people with LADA spend months or years on tablets for type 2 before anyone tests for antibodies. This guide explains what LADA is, how it's diagnosed and how it compares with type 1, type 2 and MODY.

## What LADA is

In LADA the immune system gradually attacks the cells in the pancreas that make insulin. The damage is slower than in classic type 1, so for a while the body still makes enough insulin to manage without injections. You may also hear it called "type 1.5". The American Diabetes Association groups all autoimmune diabetes under type 1, while the World Health Organization lists a slowly evolving immune-mediated form among its hybrid types. Estimates put it at 2 to 12% of people with adult-onset diabetes, varying with population and the tests used.

## Symptoms

LADA has the same symptoms as any high glucose: thirst, passing a lot of urine, tiredness, blurred vision and weight loss you can't explain. They can build up slowly. Things that make doctors think of LADA include tablets that stop working sooner than expected, a body weight that doesn't fit the typical picture of type 2, and a personal or family history of autoimmune conditions.

## How it's diagnosed

Only a blood test can tell. An international expert panel describes three key criteria:

- Diabetes that starts in adulthood, after about age 30.
- Diabetes-related autoantibodies in the blood. GAD antibodies are the most common.
- No need for insulin for at least 6 months after diagnosis.

Doctors also often measure C-peptide, which shows how much insulin your body still makes. If your diagnosis doesn't feel right, it's reasonable to ask your doctor whether antibody tests make sense for you.

## LADA, type 1, type 2 and MODY compared

| | LADA | Type 1 | Type 2 | MODY |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Autoimmune, slow | Autoimmune, faster | Insulin resistance and falling insulin production | One inherited gene |
| Usual start | Adults | Any age, often young | Adults, increasingly younger | Often before 25 to 35 |
| Antibodies | Yes | Usually | No | No |
| Insulin | Often needed sooner or later | From the start | Sometimes | Depends on the gene |
| Confirmed by | Antibodies | Clinical picture, antibodies | Clinical picture | Genetic test |

## Treatment

There's no single plan, and your care team sets it. The expert consensus bases decisions largely on how much insulin you still make, and warns that LADA can progress, so your needs may change over time. Many people with LADA end up on insulin.

## How a glucose sensor fits in

Because LADA changes slowly, a continuous glucose monitor can make those changes easier to see: higher readings after meals, or more time above range than last month. Whether a sensor is covered depends on your country and treatment, so ask your care team.

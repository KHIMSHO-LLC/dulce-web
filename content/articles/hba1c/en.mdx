HbA1c, also called A1C or glycated hemoglobin, is the blood test your diabetes team looks at first. If you wear a sensor, you also have a second number that looks almost the same: the GMI, which apps such as LibreView or Dulce show as an "estimated A1C". The two rarely match exactly, and that's expected. This guide explains what HbA1c measures, which values are normal, what can throw it off and why your sensor's estimate can sit a few tenths above or below your lab result.

<Callout type="warning">
This is general information, not medical advice. Diabetes is diagnosed by a doctor from laboratory tests, and your HbA1c target is set with your care team. Never change your treatment because of a single number, from the lab or from an app.
</Callout>

## What HbA1c measures

Glucose in your blood sticks to hemoglobin, the protein inside red blood cells. The higher your glucose over time, the larger the share of hemoglobin that ends up "glycated". Red blood cells live for a few months, so the test reflects your **average glucose over roughly the past three months**, not today's number.

That is its strength and its weakness. It's a stable, standardized lab measurement, and it's the number most diabetes targets are written in. But as an average, it can't tell calm days from days that swing between lows and highs.

## HbA1c values: normal, prediabetes, diabetes and targets

Labs report HbA1c as a percentage (NGSP) or in mmol/mol (IFCC). Many European labs print mmol/mol, sometimes both.

| HbA1c | % | mmol/mol |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal (no diabetes) | Under 5.7% | Under 39 |
| Prediabetes | 5.7-6.4% | 39-47 |
| Diabetes (confirmed by a second test) | 6.5% or more | 48 or more |
| Usual target for many adults with diabetes | Under 7% | Under 53 |
| Less strict target for some people | Under 8% | Under 64 |

The first three rows are the American Diabetes Association's diagnostic cut-offs for non-pregnant adults. The last two are goals for people who already live with diabetes, and they are individual: a stricter target can make sense if it's reachable without lows, a looser one for people with frequent severe lows, advanced complications or other serious health conditions. Pregnancy and children have their own targets. For fasting glucose and the other lab values, see our <ArticleLink to="blood-sugar-levels">blood sugar levels chart</ArticleLink>.

An HbA1c of 7% corresponds to an estimated average glucose of about 154 mg/dL (8.5 mmol/L), according to the ADAG study. Our <PageLink to="/tools/a1c-calculator">A1C and GMI calculator</PageLink> has the full chart from 4% to 14%.

## How often, and do you need to fast?

- **No fasting.** The test can be done at any time of day.
- **How often:** the US National Institute of Diabetes (NIDDK) suggests at least twice a year for people with diabetes, and more often when treatment changes or goals aren't being met. Your team may schedule it every three months.

## When HbA1c can be misleading

Because the test depends on red blood cells, anything that changes how long they live or what kind of hemoglobin you have can move the result without your glucose changing. The NIDDK lists:

- recent blood loss or a blood transfusion
- iron-deficiency anemia, which can make HbA1c falsely high
- sickle cell disease and other hemoglobin variants (more common in people of African, Mediterranean or Southeast Asian descent; not every lab method is affected)
- kidney failure, hemodialysis or treatment with erythropoietin
- liver disease

HbA1c is also less useful in pregnancy, where early results can reflect glucose from before the pregnancy. If one of these applies to you, your care team may lean more on sensor data or use a different lab method.

## HbA1c vs GMI: why your sensor's number differs

**GMI** (glucose management indicator) is not a blood test. It's a formula that turns your sensor's average glucose into an A1C-like percentage: GMI (%) = 3.31 + 0.02392 × average glucose in mg/dL. An average of 154 mg/dL gives a GMI of 7.0%.

It used to be called "estimated A1C". It was renamed in 2018 because the US FDA worried that name suggested a closer link to the lab test than there really is, and because so many people were confused when the two didn't match. In the study that defined GMI, 528 people had both values measured. How far apart they were:

| Difference between GMI and lab HbA1c | Share of people |
| --- | --- |
| Less than 0.1 points (practically the same) | 19% |
| 0.3 points or more | 51% |
| 0.5 points or more | 28% |
| 1 point or more | 3% |

So if your sensor says 7.0% and the lab says 7.4%, that's a common result, not a sign that either is broken. Reasons include the red blood cell factors above, which affect HbA1c but not the sensor, and the fact that the two cover different periods: GMI covers the days you choose, HbA1c roughly three months.

GMI needs enough data to mean anything. The study authors recommend at least 10 days of sensor data, ideally 14 or more, and the international time-in-range consensus asks for 14 days with the sensor worn at least 70% of the time.

<Callout>
If your GMI and your HbA1c are consistently far apart, tell your care team. Knowing that your lab result runs higher or lower than your sensor average helps them read both numbers correctly.
</Callout>

## High readings but a normal HbA1c, or the other way round

Two searches come up again and again, and the explanation is the same: HbA1c is an average.

- **"My glucose is high sometimes but my HbA1c is normal."** Short rises after meals pull the average up only a little, so HbA1c can stay normal. In a study of healthy people without diabetes wearing a sensor, glucose spent about 30 minutes a day above 140 mg/dL.
- **"I have lows but my HbA1c is high."** Lows and highs average out. Someone who swings between 60 and 250 mg/dL can have the same HbA1c as someone who stays near 150.

That's why sensor users also look at <ArticleLink to="time-in-range">time in range</ArticleLink>: as a rough guide, the consensus links 70% of the time between 70 and 180 mg/dL to an HbA1c of about 7%, and it shows the lows an average hides. If your lows happen at night, read our guide to <ArticleLink to="night-lows">nocturnal hypoglycemia</ArticleLink>, and check any worrying reading with your sensor or a fingerstick before acting on it.

## Your estimated A1C in Dulce

Dulce shows your FreeStyle Libre or Dexcom glucose on the Lock Screen, Apple Watch and widgets, and keeps the readings it receives on your phone. The **Trends** tab turns them into three cards for 7, 14, 30 or 90 days: time in range, average glucose and **Est. A1C**, labeled **from GMI** because that's exactly what it is, calculated with the formula above. When the previous period has enough readings, each card also shows the change since then.

A few things to keep in mind:

- It only counts the readings Dulce has saved since you connected your sensor, so a 90-day view needs 90 days of use.
- Use 14 days or more for a figure worth comparing with your lab result.
- Expect a gap with your HbA1c, as in the table above. Use the trend (going up or down) more than the exact number.

How to read the rest of the tab is in the <ArticleLink to="trends">Trends guide</ArticleLink>. If your report uses mmol/L, the <PageLink to="/tools/glucose-converter">mg/dL to mmol/L converter</PageLink> translates any value.

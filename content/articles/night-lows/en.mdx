A low during the day is unpleasant, but you usually notice it: you feel shaky, you check, you treat it. A low at 3 a.m. is different. You may sleep right through it and only find out the next morning from a strange line on the graph, a damp pillow or a headache. This guide explains why night lows are harder to catch, how to tell a real one from a sensor artifact, and how to set up your iPhone so an alert can actually wake you.

<Callout type="warning">
This is general information, not medical advice. Dulce is not a medical device and doesn't replace your sensor's alarms: keep the FreeStyle Libre app's alarms on. If night lows keep happening, talk to your care team; they are the ones who adjust your treatment.
</Callout>

## What counts as a low

The American Diabetes Association uses three levels, and they are the same at night as during the day:

| Level | Glucose | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Level 1 | Under 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) and 54 mg/dL or more | Alert value: time to act |
| Level 2 | Under 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) | Clinically significant low |
| Level 3 | Any number | A severe event: you need someone else's help to recover |

For people using a sensor, the international consensus on time in range sets a general goal of **less than 4% of the time below 70 mg/dL** and **less than 1% below 54 mg/dL**. That's about an hour a day and 15 minutes a day respectively, spread over day and night. Your care team may set stricter or looser targets for you. More on these numbers in <ArticleLink to="time-in-range">time in range vs. A1C</ArticleLink>.

## Why nights are different

During sleep, the body's alarm system for lows is weaker. In a study published in *Diabetes*, adults with type 1 diabetes released less adrenaline in response to a low while asleep than while awake, something not seen in people without diabetes, and they stayed asleep through it more often. A later study in *PLoS Medicine* made the point even more clearly: glucose was lowered during sleep in 16 people with type 1 diabetes and 16 without. Only one person with diabetes woke up, compared with ten of the people without it.

In other words: you can't count on your body to wake you. That's why it's worth checking, before you go to sleep, that an alarm can actually reach you.

## Signs you may have had one

If you don't wear a sensor overnight, or the alarm didn't go off, some people notice clues the next morning: sheets or pyjamas wet with sweat, vivid dreams or nightmares, a headache on waking, or feeling unusually tired or foggy. None of these prove a low on their own. The sensor graph is the best evidence: look at the night in the Libre app or in Dulce.

## Real low or compression low?

Sensors measure glucose in the fluid under the skin. If you lie on your sensor, the pressure can push that fluid away and the reading drops even though your blood glucose hasn't. A study in the *Journal of Diabetes Science and Technology* recorded people's sleeping position on video and found that sudden drops in sensor readings lined up with lying on the sensor.

A compression low usually looks like this:

- a **sharp, almost vertical drop** rather than a gradual slide,
- often while you sleep on the side or arm where the sensor is,
- and a **quick bounce back** to where you were once you turn over.

If it doesn't look like that, or you're unsure, treat it as real and check with a fingerstick meter if you have one. Never ignore a low reading just because it might be a compression artifact. And always confirm with your sensor or meter before acting.

## Setting up Dulce for the night

Everything below is under **Settings → Alerts** in Dulce. The full tour is in the <ArticleLink to="alerts">glucose alerts guide</ArticleLink>; these are the settings that matter at night.

<Steps>
<Step title="Keep Critical low alerts on">
It fires when glucose is 55 mg/dL or lower, always with the **Alarm** sound, and repeats every 5 minutes while you stay that low. Quiet hours and snooze don't stop it. It's on by default, and Dulce asks you to confirm before turning it off.
</Step>
<Step title="Make sure your iPhone can make a sound">
Dulce's alerts arrive as regular iPhone notifications, so they follow your phone's sound settings: with the iPhone on silent they won't ring. At night, keep silent mode off. If you use the Sleep Focus, open the iPhone Settings app → Focus → Sleep and add Dulce to the apps allowed to notify you.
</Step>
<Step title="Decide what quiet hours should mute">
**Quiet hours** mute every alert except Critical low during the window you choose. That includes the **Low glucose alert** (70 mg/dL by default, adjustable from 50 to 80) and **Predictive low**. So with quiet hours on, the only alert you'll get overnight is for a reading of 55 or below. If you'd rather hear about a low earlier, leave quiet hours off. They are off by default.
</Step>
<Step title="Consider Predictive low">
It warns you when your glucose has been falling steadily over several readings and, at that pace, would cross your low threshold within about 15 minutes. It only suggests checking your sensor, never a treatment. It's off by default.
</Step>
</Steps>

Alerts don't depend on the app being open: Dulce's server checks your LibreLinkUp readings every minute with Dulce Pro and every 5 minutes with the free version. If you sleep with an Apple Watch, iOS sends the alert to your wrist when the iPhone is locked. And keep the Libre app's own alarms on as well: two independent alarms are better than one.

## When someone else watches over the night

Parents of children with type 1 diabetes, partners and adult children of older parents often want to know about a night low too. Followers in Dulce get their own alerts, with their own quiet hours, and very low readings always reach them. The Live Activity on their Lock Screen shows the number without unlocking the phone, which helps on a 3 a.m. check. See <ArticleLink to="child-glucose">how to see your child's glucose on your iPhone</ArticleLink>.

## Look for patterns, not single nights

One low night happens. The same low at the same hour, night after night, is a pattern worth taking to your next appointment. In Dulce, the **Trends** tab splits your time in range by **Time of day**, including the night. Look at 14 days rather than 7, and check time below range before anything else. The <ArticleLink to="trends">Trends guide</ArticleLink> explains each chart.

Exercise is one known factor: an international consensus on exercise in type 1 diabetes notes that it can raise the risk of a low the following night, especially when the activity is late in the day. What to change is a decision for you and your care team, not for an app.

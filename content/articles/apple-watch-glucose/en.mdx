Short answer: no. No Apple Watch can measure your blood sugar, including the Series 12 that came out in September 2026. Apple has been working on it for years, and the latest reports say it's still years away. If you want your glucose on your wrist today, you need a glucose sensor (a CGM) and an app that shows its readings on the watch. Here's what Apple Watch measures, what Apple is reportedly building, which "glucose watches" to avoid, and what works right now.

## What Apple Watch measures today

Apple Watch reads your heart rate and can take an ECG, measure blood oxygen and, on recent models, notify you about possible sleep apnea or signs of high blood pressure. Those are all optical or electrical signals from the skin. Glucose isn't one of them: nothing in the Series 12, the Ultra or the SE measures it, and Apple doesn't claim it does.

What Apple Watch *can* do is **show** glucose measured by something else. The Health app stores blood glucose from sensors and meters, and apps for continuous glucose monitors put the reading on the watch face.

## Is Apple working on blood sugar measurement?

Yes, according to Bloomberg's Mark Gurman, and for a long time: the project has reportedly been running for more than 15 years. What has been reported:

- **How it would work**: a laser sends specific wavelengths of light under the skin, into the interstitial fluid around the cells, and a sensor reads how much light comes back. An algorithm turns that into a glucose estimate. (Gurman, 2023.)
- **Early use**: the reports talk about the watch warning people who may have prediabetes, not about precise numbers.
- **Latest news (May 2026)**: Apple moved the project to a new leader, Zongjian Chen, which some read as a sign it's progressing. Even so, the reporting says the feature is unlikely to arrive for several more years, if ever.

Apple hasn't announced anything itself. Treat any date you read as a rumor.

## Why it's so hard

Glucose is a tiny signal. Under the skin it sits next to water, fat, proteins and blood flow, and the reading also shifts with temperature, sweat, skin tone and how tight the band is. Getting a reliable number from light alone, through the skin, all day, is a problem that many companies have tried and none has solved at the accuracy people with diabetes need.

And for someone who takes insulin, "roughly right" isn't enough. A reading that's off can lead to the wrong dose. That's why a wrist sensor would need to match today's sensors and be cleared by regulators like the FDA before anyone could treat with it.

## Beware of watches and rings that "measure blood sugar"

Online shops sell cheap smartwatches and rings that claim to show glucose without a needle. In February 2024 the FDA warned people not to buy or use them: none has been authorized, they don't actually test blood glucose, and wrong readings can lead to dangerous dosing errors.

<Callout type="warning">
If a watch or ring claims to measure blood sugar without anything under the skin, don't use it to make decisions. Use a sensor or meter your care team recommends.
</Callout>

## How to see your blood sugar on Apple Watch today

The reliable way is a **continuous glucose monitor (CGM)**, like a FreeStyle Libre or a Dexcom. It has a thin filament just under the skin that measures glucose in the interstitial fluid every few minutes and sends it to your phone. From there an app can put the reading on your watch.

That's what Dulce does. It reads your FreeStyle Libre through LibreLinkUp or your Dexcom through Dexcom Share and shows it on your Apple Watch:

- **Complications** for every watch face: rectangular (number, arrow and chart), circular, corner and inline.
- **A watch app** with the big number, the trend and the day's chart.
- **Updates even when your iPhone isn't nearby**: Dulce's server sends each new reading straight to the watch over Wi-Fi or cellular.
- Works on any Apple Watch with watchOS 11 or later, including SE (2nd generation and later) and Ultra. The watch is part of Dulce Pro, with a 14-day free trial.

## If Apple ships it one day

When a glucose feature does arrive, check two things before relying on it: whether it gives numbers or only a "you may have prediabetes" style warning, and whether it's cleared by regulators for diabetes treatment where you live. Until then, if you have diabetes, a sensor is the way to see your glucose on your wrist.

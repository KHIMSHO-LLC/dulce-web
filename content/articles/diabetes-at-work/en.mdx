Managing type 1 diabetes at work usually comes down to two questions: how do I see my glucose without stopping what I'm doing, and what do I tell my employer? The first has a practical answer that depends on your job. The second depends on where you live. This guide covers both, and it also applies to anyone on insulin with a sensor, whatever their type of diabetes.

## See your number without opening an app

Unlocking your phone, finding an app and waiting for it to load takes seconds, but twenty times a day it breaks every meeting, ward round and job on site. Dulce keeps the number where you already look. The best spot depends on your work:

| Your work | What fits |
| --- | --- |
| Office, meetings | Lock Screen Live Activity with the phone face up, or a glance at the watch |
| Hospital, care, teaching | Apple Watch: a look at your wrist with no phone out |
| Building site, factory, gloves on | Apple Watch, or the phone in a chest pocket with the Lock Screen |
| Driving for work | The CarPlay dashboard card on iOS 26, checked when you're stopped |
| Shifts and nights | Quiet hours for sleep after a night shift, with very low alerts still coming through |

The watch face and Lock Screen are explained in the <ArticleLink to="apple-watch">Apple Watch guide</ArticleLink> and the <ArticleLink to="lock-screen">Lock Screen guide</ArticleLink>. ## Shift and night work

Shifts move meals, sleep and insulin timing, so talk them through with your care team before changing patterns. In Dulce you can set quiet hours around the hours you actually sleep, and they follow your phone's time zone.

## What to tell your employer

The rules differ by country, so this isn't legal advice. In the United States, diabetes is generally covered by the Americans with Disabilities Act. The EEOC says breaks to eat, drink, check glucose or take medicine are the kind of adjustment an employer can be asked for, and that you can request one after you've started work.

Elsewhere, ask your national diabetes association, a union or an employment adviser. Jobs with safety rules, such as professional driving, work at height or machinery, can have their own requirements and medical checks, so ask before you assume either way.

Many people choose to tell at least one colleague how to recognise a low and what to do. A sensor on your arm and a buzzing phone are easier to explain when someone already knows.

## Keep your supplies close

A fast-acting carb in every bag, your locker and your car does more than any app. Keep what your care team recommends within reach and know where it is on every site you work.

<Callout type="warning">
Dulce is not a medical device and doesn't replace your sensor's alarms. Keep your eyes on the road: check the screen when you're stopped. If you feel low, stop what you're doing and treat it as your care team showed you, whatever the screen says.
</Callout>

For years the only number that mattered at the clinic was A1C. Continuous sensors brought another one: **time in range**. It doesn't measure the same thing and it doesn't replace A1C, but it answers the question we care about day to day: how many hours am I where I should be?

## What time in range is

It's the percentage of your sensor readings between **70 and 180 mg/dL** (3.9 to 10.0 mmol/L). If your time in range is 70%, you spent about 17 of the day's 24 hours inside that band.

It usually comes with two more numbers:

- **Time below range**: readings under 70 mg/dL, and separately those under 54 mg/dL.
- **Time above range**: readings over 180 mg/dL, and separately those over 250 mg/dL.

## The international consensus targets

In 2019 an international group of experts published in *Diabetes Care* the targets most care teams use today. For most adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes:

| Metric | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Time in range (70-180 mg/dL) | Over 70% (about 16 h 48 min a day) |
| Below 70 mg/dL | Under 4% (under 1 h a day) |
| Below 54 mg/dL | Under 1% (under 15 min a day) |
| Above 180 mg/dL | Under 25% (under 6 h a day) |
| Above 250 mg/dL | Under 5% (under 1 h 12 min a day) |

For older people or those at high risk of hypoglycemia, the consensus suggests more cautious targets (over 50% in range and under 1% below 70). In pregnancy with type 1 diabetes the range is tighter (63-140 mg/dL). These are general guidelines: your care team sets your targets.

The same paper notes that every **5% more time in range** already brings a clinical benefit. You don't have to jump from 50% to 70% in one go.

## Time in range vs. A1C

**A1C** is a blood test that reflects an average over about three months. Its weakness is that an average hides the extremes: two people at 7% can have calm days, or a rollercoaster of lows and highs that cancel each other out. The lab cut-offs for normal, prediabetes and diabetes are in our <ArticleLink to="blood-sugar-levels">blood sugar levels chart</ArticleLink>.

**Time in range** does see those extremes, and it moves in days, not months. As a reference point, the consensus notes that 70% time in range corresponds roughly to an A1C of 7%.

Then there's **GMI** (glucose management indicator), which works out an A1C-like number from your sensor's average glucose. You can calculate it with our <PageLink to="/tools/a1c-calculator">A1C and GMI calculator</PageLink>, where we also explain why it doesn't always match your lab result.

<Callout>
The consensus asks for at least 14 days of data, with the sensor worn 70% of the time, for these numbers to be reliable. A single day says very little.
</Callout>

## How to see it in Dulce

<Figure src="/guides/home-screen.jpg" alt="Dulce home screen showing 131 mg/dL and the time in range bar at 96% in range" caption="The time in range bar on the home screen: low, in range and high." />

Dulce shows your time in range on the home screen and, in more detail, in the **Trends** tab, where you can see it over 7, 14, 30 or 90 days and split by time of day. We walk through it in the <ArticleLink to="trends">Trends guide</ArticleLink>.

In **Settings** you can change the target range (70-180 mg/dL by default) and your time-in-range goal (70% by default), in case your care team has given you different ones.

Search for "normal blood sugar" and you get a dozen charts that don't agree with each other. Part of the reason is that they mix up three different things: the cut-offs used to **diagnose** diabetes, what glucose looks like in people **without** diabetes, and the **targets** for people who already live with it. This guide keeps them apart, with every number in mg/dL and mmol/L and where it comes from.

<Callout type="warning">
This is general information, not medical advice. Diabetes is diagnosed by a doctor from laboratory tests, not from a home meter, a sensor or a table on the internet. If you live with diabetes, your care team sets your targets, and they may differ from the ones below.
</Callout>

## The chart: normal, prediabetes and diabetes

These are the American Diabetes Association's diagnostic criteria for non-pregnant adults. They apply to lab tests on blood plasma, not to sensor readings.

| Test | Normal | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fasting glucose | Under 100 mg/dL (under 5.6 mmol/L) | 100-125 mg/dL (5.6-6.9 mmol/L) | 126 mg/dL or more (7.0 mmol/L or more) |
| 2 hours into a glucose tolerance test | Under 140 mg/dL (under 7.8 mmol/L) | 140-199 mg/dL (7.8-11.0 mmol/L) | 200 mg/dL or more (11.1 mmol/L or more) |
| HbA1c | Under 5.7% (under 39 mmol/mol) | 5.7-6.4% (39-47 mmol/mol) | 6.5% or more (48 mmol/mol or more) |
| Random glucose, with classic symptoms | | | 200 mg/dL or more (11.1 mmol/L or more) |

A few details that charts usually leave out:

- **Fasting** means no food or caloric drinks for at least 8 hours.
- **The tolerance test** (OGTT) is not "two hours after lunch". It's a lab test where you drink 75 g of glucose and get measured two hours later.
- **One result isn't a diagnosis.** Unless the picture is unmistakable, the ADA asks for two abnormal results, either from the same sample or from two separate tests.
- **Not everyone draws the prediabetes line at 100.** The World Health Organization uses the same diabetes cut-offs, but its "impaired fasting glucose" starts at 110 mg/dL (6.1 mmol/L). So a fasting 105 mg/dL can be called normal on one lab report and prediabetes on another.

## What "normal" looks like hour by hour

Diagnostic cut-offs say little about how glucose moves during a day. For that, a 2019 study put a continuous glucose sensor on 153 healthy people without diabetes, aged 7 to 80, for up to 10 days. What it found:

- Average glucose was around **98-99 mg/dL (5.4-5.5 mmol/L)**, and about 104 mg/dL in people over 60.
- They spent a median **96% of the time between 70 and 140 mg/dL** (3.9-7.8 mmol/L).
- They spent about **30 minutes a day above 140** and about **15 minutes below 70**.

So a short rise after a meal is normal, even without diabetes. Staying above 140 mg/dL for hours is not what this group's days looked like.

## Targets if you have diabetes

For many non-pregnant adults with diabetes, the ADA suggests:

| What | Target |
| --- | --- |
| HbA1c | Under 7% (under 53 mmol/mol) |
| Before meals | 80-130 mg/dL (4.4-7.2 mmol/L) |
| Peak after meals (1-2 h after you start eating) | Under 180 mg/dL (under 10.0 mmol/L) |
| Time in range with a sensor (70-180 mg/dL) | Over 70% of the day |

The ADA is explicit that these goals are a starting point. They can be stricter if they're reachable without lows, and looser for people for whom lows are a bigger risk, such as many older adults with other health conditions. Children and pregnancy have their own targets. Your care team sets yours.

With a sensor, time in range has become as important as HbA1c because it shows the lows and highs an average hides. We explain the full set of sensor targets in <ArticleLink to="time-in-range">time in range vs. A1C</ArticleLink>.

## Low and high: where the lines are

The ADA classifies lows in three levels, the same day and night:

| Level | Glucose |
| --- | --- |
| Level 1 | Under 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) and 54 mg/dL or more |
| Level 2 | Under 54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L) |
| Level 3 | A severe event where you need someone else's help, at any number |

On the high side, the time-in-range consensus counts readings over **180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L)** as above range and over **250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L)** as very high, with a general goal of under 5% of the time up there.

If a reading looks wrong for how you feel, confirm it with your sensor or a fingerstick meter, and act on the plan you've agreed with your care team, not on a chart. Lows during sleep are their own topic: see <ArticleLink to="night-lows">nocturnal hypoglycemia</ArticleLink>.

## HbA1c in % and mmol/mol

Labs report HbA1c either as a percentage (NGSP) or in mmol/mol (IFCC), and some print both. The two are linked by a fixed equation:

| % | mmol/mol |
| --- | --- |
| 5.7 | 39 |
| 6.0 | 42 |
| 6.5 | 48 |
| 7.0 | 53 |
| 7.5 | 58 |
| 8.0 | 64 |

To turn an HbA1c into an estimated average glucose, or a sensor average into an estimated A1C (GMI), use our <PageLink to="/tools/a1c-calculator">A1C and GMI calculator</PageLink>.

## mg/dL or mmol/L

Same glucose, two units. To go from mg/dL to mmol/L, divide by 18 (precisely 18.016); to go back, multiply. 100 mg/dL is 5.6 mmol/L, 180 mg/dL is 10.0 mmol/L. Spain, Italy, France and the US mostly use mg/dL; the UK, Ireland, Canada and Australia use mmol/L; in Germany you'll see both. If you're in Canada, one more difference besides the unit: Diabetes Canada uses the same diabetes cut-offs as the ADA but starts prediabetes higher, at a fasting 6.1-6.9 mmol/L or an A1C of 6.0-6.4%. For any other number there's the <PageLink to="/tools/glucose-converter">mg/dL to mmol/L converter</PageLink>.

## Your own range in Dulce

Dulce shows FreeStyle Libre and Dexcom readings on your Lock Screen, Apple Watch and widgets, so it helps if those numbers are in the unit and range you actually use.

<Figure src="/guides/home-screen.jpg" alt="Dulce home screen showing 131 mg/dL and the time in range bar at 96% in range" caption="The in-range coloring on the home screen follows your target range." />

<Steps>
<Step title="Pick your unit">
In **Settings → Units** choose mg/dL or mmol/L. It changes every number in the app, on the Lock Screen and in the widgets.
</Step>
<Step title="Set your target range">
**Settings → Target range** is 70-180 mg/dL by default. You can move the low end between 50 and 150 and the high end between 120 and 300, in steps of 5 mg/dL. It sets the in-range coloring on Home and in Trends.
</Step>
<Step title="Set your time-in-range goal">
On the same screen, **Time-in-range goal** is 70% by default and goes from 50% to 95%. Use the numbers your care team gave you.
</Step>
</Steps>

The **Trends** tab then shows your time in range and an **Est. A1C** from GMI over 7, 14, 30 or 90 days, as explained in the <ArticleLink to="trends">Trends guide</ArticleLink>. GMI is an estimate and doesn't replace a lab HbA1c.
